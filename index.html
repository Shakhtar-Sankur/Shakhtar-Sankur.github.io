GIGZEN

Incorporated 2026. One company, two products, one thesis.

The people who move the world’s goods have no platform of their own.

Gigzen is the company I co-founded. It exists to build for the 1.57 billion people who work the gig economy — drivers, riders, couriers, freelancers — who between them power a $582 billion market growing at nearly 16% a year, and who have no professional home the way office workers have LinkedIn.

Two products came out of it. Buzz Buzz is the platform itself: free for workers, forever, funded by enterprises rather than by the people using it. Populace is the testing platform I had to build first, because a social product with one person in it cannot be tested at all. The second exists because of the first, and now stands on its own.

buzz buzz

Product 01 — global gig worker platform

1.57 billion workers. No platform built for them.

A Swiggy rider, an Uber driver and an Amazon Flex courier are often the same person — but no platform connects those identities. More than half of gig workers operate across multiple platforms simultaneously, and each platform sees only its own slice. The worker carries the fragmentation.

The consequences compound. Without a single verifiable work history, banks read gig workers as unemployed and refuse them credit and insurance, despite daily earned income.

what i built

Architecture, implementation, release engineering

The bug worth the story. Chat reads failed silently across the whole app. A row-level-security policy on thread membership recursed into itself — the policy had to read the table it was guarding. Fixed with a SECURITY DEFINER helper that breaks the cycle, then verified end to end against a live project with two real accounts.

The number worth measuring. Photos were stored as base64 inside a Postgres column. Measured: 479 KB a picture, and up to 9.4 MB to open a feed of twenty — on a driver’s metered data, and in every database backup. Moved to object storage with a thumbnail; the feed now loads about 20 KB an image and fetches the full one only when someone taps it.

React and TypeScript on Capacitor over a Supabase backend. Realtime sync, WhatsApp-style read receipts, presence, push notifications, photo posts, groups, and full localisation into 16 languages including right-to-left Arabic. Writes survive a lost signal: a post or message sent underground is queued on the device and sent when the connection returns. Signed for release and tested end to end against a live backend.

populace

Product 02 — simulated populations for testing

A social app with nobody in it tells you nothing.

You cannot test presence, live sync, read receipts, notification fan-out, or “does deleting this remove it for everyone” while being only one person. I hit that wall building Buzz Buzz, so I built the thing that solves it.

Populace gives you dozens of believable people who sign up, move around, post, comment, message each other and join groups — as real authenticated users, through your own API, with your own permission rules applying. Then it hands you a report on what broke.

The decision worth asking about. A fresh adapter has all 13 methods — as empty stubs. Naive coverage reports 13/13 and passes while testing nothing. So an empty body deliberately doesn’t count: a new scaffold honestly reports 2/13 and refuses to run. A reporting tool that manufactures confidence is worse than no reporting tool.

What it found on its first real run. Three of the five bugs were the same mistake in different clothes: the app used upsert, and INSERT…ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE needs SELECT on every column it touches — one of which had deliberately been made unreadable that morning to protect phone numbers. Signup, profile edits and repeat likes all failed silently.

The other two were in Populace’s own reference adapter, and the worse one was an unchecked error — the exact fault this tool exists to catch. It reported a signup failure against a later call, turning one cause into three symptoms. Fixing one line changed the report from “post failed 14 times” to “profile row not created: permission denied”.

01

App-agnostic engine

The core knows how to be a person and contains zero references to any real app. Everything specific lives behind a 13-method adapter.

02

Three production guards

Environment must declare itself non-production, a denylist is matched against every nested string, and an empty denylist warns loudly.

03

Honest coverage

Anything you skip is reported as untested rather than quietly passing. Empty method bodies never count as coverage.

04

Per-endpoint latency

Every call through the adapter is timed and counted, with p50, p95 and p99 per method, and failures grouped by shape rather than by text.

05

Session refresh

Runs outlive token expiry. Without it an expired run cannot even delete its own accounts, stranding simulated users in your environment.

06

Cleans up after itself

Every account a run creates is removed, and the report states plainly whether cleanup succeeded.

how the two
fit together

Populace was built to test Buzz Buzz, and its engine was deliberately kept free of any reference to it. That single constraint is what turned an internal script into a product: Buzz Buzz became its first customer rather than its purpose. Anyone building something multiplayer has the same problem, and can point Populace at their own API by writing one adapter.

Proven against a real app

populace

Pointed at Buzz Buzz’s live backend on 9 August 2026 — an app that had already passed a full manual test of every screen. Six simulated drivers across Manila and Mumbai found five bugs in three and a half minutes, including one that stopped account creation outright: a privacy change had made a column unreadable, and the signup write silently needed to read it. After the fixes, no failures across 400 API calls, six accounts created and six removed.

Two of the five were in Populace’s own reference adapter, and the worse one was an unchecked error — the exact fault this tool exists to catch. It made the report blame a later call for a failure that happened during signup.

Hardened since, by running it on genuinely bad infrastructure rather than a clean desk. Three defects surfaced that way: a single unresponsive call could freeze an entire run and produce nothing; fixing that with retries made a dead target grind for twenty minutes instead; and the circuit breaker that fixed that was still open at teardown, so it left five simulated accounts live in a real project. All three are fixed and covered — 59 self-tests, up from 20. A report now separates a failure in your code from a failure of the network in between, and says inconclusive rather than clean when it could not prove anything.

Next: a run against someone else’s app, not our own.
